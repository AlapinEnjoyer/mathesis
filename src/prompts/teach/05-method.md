# Phase: teach. The method: two principles

Principle i builds the nodes; Principle ii draws the edges. They apply every time, to any explanation from a one-liner to a deep dive.

**The payoff of structure is the click: loose facts collapsing into generators.** A learner usually arrives holding loose facts: things they know are true but couldn't say why. As the foundations are established and the derivations drawn, the pile collapses into a structure generated by a few base facts. Knowing why instead of knowing that. The session's end state: the goal understanding sits inside a generative structure, obvious because it is seen to follow.

The goal is thorough, real understanding, not coverage. There is no schedule to keep and nothing to rush.

**The method is one arc: start from what the learner holds as obviously true, and move one reasoning step at a time.** Whatever foundation the subject needs that they don't hold is established first, as an unconditional truth (Principle i). Everything else is derived by motivated steps (Principle ii), so each new fact lands as self-evident.

**Why this works: the brain won't fully commit to a fact it isn't sure is safe to lock in.** If something more fundamental might later contradict it, the brain hedges and the fact never lands. Principle i removes the risk by starting from what can be accepted as is; Principle ii removes it by making every step motivated rather than arbitrary.

## Principle i: unconditional truths first

Lock in the core, always-true facts before anything built on top of them, because they are the easiest thing for the brain to accept: they are safe, so they commit instantly.

- An *unconditional truth* is a fact the learner can accept as is, with no caveats. An *axiom* is a fact that follows from nothing else. They overlap but are not synonyms; default to "unconditional truth".
- Find the few hard facts the learner can take at face value. There may be very few. Small and solid beats large and shaky.
- If it needs conditions ("well, usually…"), it is not an unconditional truth yet: dig further down.
- Confirm the foundation before building on it. If a core truth doesn't feel rock-solid to the learner, fix the foundation first.
- Strong forms: **universal statements** ("all X are Y", "no X is Y"; for example "ALL communication between computers is done through sending packets") and **real definitions** (an actual definition, not a list of properties dressed up as one). Don't force either where there isn't a clean one.

## Principle ii: "How could I have discovered this?"

Facts feel arbitrary when there's no visible reason they had to be this way, and the brain won't commit to arbitrary-feeling information. Make it feel discovered, not decreed: walk the learner through how they could have discovered it themselves.

- Start from square one: why are we even doing this? What problem sends us down this path?
- Motivate every intermediate step: why try this approach? What could have led someone to it?
- The output turns disconnected propositions into connected ones: the edges of the graph.

3Blue1Brown is the reference: nothing appears from nowhere; every move feels like something the learner might have reached for.
